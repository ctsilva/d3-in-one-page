Color for visualization

Color is the channel people notice first and read least precisely. This lab takes apart the choices behind every colored chart: what the color is for, which space to build it in, which kind of scale fits the data, and whether everyone who looks at it can read it.

This page follows Marks, channels and graphs and uses the same setup: the 495 Skubal pitches in pitches, and the helpers chart, row, swatches, ramp, tooltip and strikeZone, described in section 2 of that page. Two more helpers are added here: locationBins, introduced in section 4, and cvd, introduced in section 8.

How to use this page

As before, every code box is live: edit, then Run or ⌘/Ctrl + Enter. Each example also has a View as menu, which re-renders the output as it appears to someone with one of the three kinds of dichromacy, or in grayscale. Use it on every example, not just in section 8. (It works in Chrome, Edge and Firefox; Safari may ignore it.) Expect about two and a half hours.

1Two jobs for color

Color does one of two jobs in a chart, and each needs a different kind of scale.

  • Label: say which category a mark belongs to. The colors should be as different from each other as possible, with none standing out more than the others. The difference is mostly in hue.
  • Quantify: say how much. The colors should form an obvious order, with equal steps in the data looking like equal steps in color. The order is carried mostly by lightness.

Below, pitch locations colored both ways. On the left, hue labels the pitch type; on the right, a sequential scale shows release speed.

Each chart does its own job and fails at the other. On the left you can find the changeups instantly, but you cannot tell which dots are fast. On the right the fast pitches up in the zone stand out, but you cannot pick out sliders from changeups, because their speeds overlap. Before choosing colors, decide which question the color should answer.

2Color spaces

A color space is a way of naming a color with three numbers. D3 supports several, and converts between them.

  • RGB (d3.rgb): how much red, green and blue light the screen emits. How displays work, not how people see.
  • HSL (d3.hsl): hue, saturation, lightness. A rearrangement of RGB that is easier to reason about, but its "lightness" is not the lightness you perceive.
  • CIELAB (d3.lab): L* is perceived lightness from 0 (black) to 100 (white); a* and b* are the red–green and yellow–blue opponent axes of human vision. Designed so equal distances look roughly like equal differences.
  • HCL (d3.hcl): the same space in polar form: hue angle, chroma (colorfulness) and luminance L*. The most convenient space for designing color scales.

HSL lightness is not lightness

Six colors, all with HSL saturation 100% and lightness 50%. Under each is the grey with the same CIELAB lightness L*, which is what the color contributes to contrast and legibility.

Yellow is nearly white in lightness and blue is nearly black, although HSL calls them equal. This is why yellow text on white is unreadable, why pure blue on black is hard to see, and why a rainbow scale has a bright band in the middle that the eye reads as a feature of the data.

Use d3.hcl(h, 60, 65) instead of d3.hsl(h, 1, 0.5): hue varies, chroma and luminance are fixed. How do the greys compare now? Why do some of these colors look duller than the HSL ones? (Hint: not every combination of hue, chroma and lightness can be shown on a screen, and D3 clips the ones that cannot.)
One solution

With HCL the greys are nearly identical: L* 65 for four of the colors and 67 for the other two. Those two, the teal at hue 180 and the blue at 240, are marked "clipped": chroma 60 at that lightness is outside what an sRGB screen can show, so D3 clamps the channels, and that nudges their lightness. The HCL colors look duller than the HSL ones because they are: fully saturated HSL colors reach much higher chroma, but only by letting lightness swing from 30 to 98. You can have equal lightness or maximum colorfulness across hues, not both.

Interpolating between two colors

A continuous color scale blends between colors, and the space the blend happens in changes the result. Under each strip is its L* along the way. Pick your own endpoints with the color inputs.

RGB gives a greyish, washed-out middle. HSL travels around the hue wheel, through bright greens that belong to neither endpoint, and its lightness climbs unevenly and overshoots the end. Lab and HCL make lightness change smoothly from one end to the other. HCL also keeps colorfulness up, but it too walks around the hue wheel, so it can pass through unrelated hues. For scales that show quantities, interpolate in Lab or HCL, or better, use the ready-made scales in the next sections, which were designed with these problems in mind.

Set the endpoints to a dark blue and a light yellow. Which interpolation gives the steadiest rise in L*? Now set both endpoints to colors with the same lightness, for example red #d45d57 and green #46943a, both L* 55. What happens to the L* line, and what does that mean for a scale built from those two colors?
Discussion

From #08306b to #ffffcc, the largest gap between each L* line and a straight line from start to end is 0.2 for Lab, 1.0 for HCL, 1.7 for RGB and 33 for HSL. Lab gives the steadiest rise, and RGB is close only because these endpoints happen to differ mostly in lightness; HSL's detour around the hue wheel throws its lightness far off.

With #d45d57 and #46943a, both at L* 55, the Lab and HCL lines are flat, and the RGB line sags in the middle, which is the muddy brown. A flat L* line means the scale has no lightness order at all: in grayscale every value looks the same, and the difference between the ends rests entirely on hue. For a reader with deuteranopia those two ends are only ΔE 6 apart. Never build a scale for quantities from two colors of equal lightness.

3Categorical color

For labels, you want colors that are easy to tell apart and roughly equal in visual weight, so that no category looks more important than the others. In practice that means different hues with similar lightness and chroma. D3 includes the well-tested palettes from ColorBrewer and Tableau; you rarely need to invent one.

Palettes whose dots cluster, such as Tableau10, Observable10 and Dark2, have even visual weight. Set1 has a yellow near L* 100, which will vanish on a white background, next to much darker reds, blues and purples. Pastel1 is uniformly light, which is fine for large areas and too faint for small dots. Paired is built from light–dark pairs for data that come in pairs, such as before and after.

Trying palettes on real marks

A palette only matters on a chart. Change the palette with the menu, and check each one with the View as menu under the code, especially the last option, which uses red and green for neighboring categories.

The pitch types here form separate clusters, so even a poor palette is partly rescued by position. Colors are hardest to tell apart when marks are small, intermixed and far apart on the page. Judge a palette on the hardest chart it will be used in.

How many categories?

Beyond about six to eight hues, people stop recognizing colors at a glance and start matching each mark against the legend. The field description has nine values. The fix is rarely a bigger palette; it is usually fewer, more meaningful groups.

On the left, try to find the called strikes: you have to check the legend, then hunt. On the right the structure is visible without effort: balls surround the zone, called strikes and contact fill it, and many swinging strikes are outside it, where batters chased. Grouping is a data decision that makes the color decision easy. The four colors on the right come from the Okabe–Ito palette, designed to stay distinct for viewers with color vision deficiencies.

Some pitches went badly for Skubal: the ones hit hard. Add a fifth group, "hard contact", for pitches with launch_speed >= 95, and give it a color that stands out from the other four without being confused with them. Check your choice with View as.
One solution

34 pitches were hit at 95 mph or more. Black is the simplest choice: it is far from all four other colors for every kind of vision (ΔE above 55), and it stands out without competing hue. The remaining Okabe–Ito colors also work: reddish purple #cc79a7 stays at least ΔE 18 from the others under every simulation. Two traps: another blue would be confused with "called strike", and yellow #f0e442 is nearly invisible on white. Drawing the group last, and slightly larger, matters as much as the color.

Emphasis: use color sparingly

Every saturated color competes for attention. If the chart has one message, give color to that message and grey to the rest. Here is the share of each pitch type's pitches that got a swing and a miss, three ways.

4Sequential color

A sequential scale maps low to high values onto light to dark colors (or dark to light on a dark background). Its essential property is that lightness changes monotonically: always in the same direction, with no reversals, so the order of the colors is obvious without a legend. Hue may change along the way, which adds discriminability, as long as lightness keeps going the same way.

The examples in this section use a new helper, locationBins(rows, size), which bins pitches into square cells over the strike zone area, as example 8.2 of the previous page did by hand. Each cell has its bounds and its rows.

Greys and Blues read instantly: darker means more. Viridis changes hue as well as lightness, which lets you tell more levels apart. The rainbow is the familiar failure. Its hue order has to be learned from the legend, and d3.interpolateRainbow is also cyclical: it starts and ends at the same red, so the emptiest and the fullest cells get nearly the same color. Try View as grayscale on this example: the first three keep their order and the rainbow loses it.

Look at the lightness

You can check any color scale by plotting its L* from one end to the other. A good sequential scale gives a steady, straight-ish line.

The single-hue ColorBrewer scales (Greys, Blues) and the multi-hue YlOrRd fall steadily. Viridis, Cividis and Magma rise steadily and were designed to be perceptually uniform; Cividis was also designed to look nearly the same to viewers with red–green deficiencies. Turbo, an improved rainbow, goes up and then down: two data values far apart can share a lightness. The rainbow's L* wanders up and back down.

False edges

The worst effect of a rainbow is on smooth data. Below is a smooth estimate of pitch density: a Gaussian bump at every pitch, summed on a fine grid. It has no edges. The rainbow draws some anyway, where the hue passes quickly through bright cyan and yellow.

On the right, the jump from green to red around the zone looks like a boundary, and the blue center looks like a hollow, although it is the highest point of the surface. Neither exists. Viridis shows the same surface as what it is, a single smooth hill.

Replace the rainbow with d3.interpolateTurbo. Is it better? Where does it still suggest structure that is not there? Then try d3.interpolateGreys and d3.interpolateMagma, and decide which you would publish.
One solution

Turbo is better than the rainbow: it runs from low to high without wrapping around, so the peak and the empty edges no longer share a color. But its lightness rises and then falls (example 4.2): the lightest part of the image is the yellow-green ring halfway up the hill, and the actual peak is a darker red. The brightest feature is a ring around the peak, not the peak itself. Greys and Magma both show one hill with the darkest or brightest point at the top. For publication, Magma or Viridis: perceptually even, readable in grayscale, and with enough hue change to tell more levels apart than Greys.

5Diverging color

A diverging scale has two sequential arms that meet at a light, neutral midpoint. Use it when the data has a meaningful middle value and both directions away from it matter: above or below average, gain or loss, more strikes than balls or fewer. d3.scaleDiverging takes a three-value domain, [low, middle, high].

A meaningful midpoint

For each location cell with at least three pitches, the share of pitches that were strikes (anything but a ball: called and swinging strikes, fouls, and balls put in play). Fifty percent is a natural middle: above it the location favors the pitcher.

Both charts show the same numbers. Only the diverging one answers "where does a pitch become more likely to be a strike than a ball?" at a glance, and its answer roughly traces the strike zone: blue in and around it, red farther out. On the sequential chart, 45% and 55% are two nearly identical blues.

Do not use a diverging scale just because it looks richer. If the data has no meaningful middle, such as pitch counts or speeds, the neutral midpoint will make readers think values near it are "normal" and the arms are "unusual" in two opposite ways. That is a claim the data does not support.

Keep the arms balanced

Each game and inning below shows how much faster or slower his fastball was than his average over all five games. Zero is the midpoint. The data runs from −1.4 to +0.9 mph, and there are two ways to set the domain.

With the data's extent as the domain, each arm is stretched to its own maximum, so +0.9 is as dark a blue as −1.4 is a red. The reader compares colors and concludes the fast innings were as extreme as the slow ones. The symmetric domain gives equal differences equal colors. Note also the numbers: several cells average fewer than five pitches, so no color here should be trusted far. Hover to see the counts.

RdBu uses red for "below". In baseball, a slower fastball may be a warning sign, but red–blue also suggests cold and hot, or political parties. Try d3.interpolatePuOr and d3.interpolateBrBG. Which reads most neutrally? Which would you use for a pitching coach?
One solution

There is no single right answer. PuOr and BrBG read most neutrally: purple, orange, brown and teal carry no strong good–bad or hot–cold meaning, and both stay distinct for readers with red–green deficiencies. For a pitching coach, whose question is "when did his velocity drop?", a scheme where slower is the warm, alarming end fits the task, and RdBu does that; keep it, but say in the title that red means slower. The point of the exercise is that a color scale carries associations along with the data, and you should pick the associations on purpose.

6Binned color scales

A continuous scale gives every value its own color, which the eye cannot tell apart beyond a handful of levels anyway. A binned scale uses a few classes, each with a distinct color, so the legend can be read exactly. The choice is where to put the class breaks:

  • d3.scaleQuantize: breaks at equal intervals of the value.
  • d3.scaleQuantile: breaks at quantiles, so each class holds the same number of cells.
  • d3.scaleThreshold: breaks you choose, at values that mean something.

The three maps disagree about how much of the area is "busy". Quantize reserves its darkest class for the few cells near the maximum. Quantile spreads the non-empty cells evenly over the classes, which maximizes contrast but makes a cell with 16 pitches look like one with 28. Threshold uses round numbers a reader can remember: none, a few, some, many, very many. Whichever you choose, the legend must show the breaks.

Change the threshold breaks to [1, 3, 6, 12] and the palette to d3.schemeYlGnBu[5]. Then change the cell size in locationBins to 0.25: which of the three scales keeps its meaning when the counts per cell change?
One solution

With quarter-foot cells each cell holds a quarter of the area, and the busiest cell drops from 28 pitches to 11. Quantize and quantile rescale themselves to the new counts, so what each color means changes: the darkest quantize class used to start at 22 pitches and now starts at 9. Threshold is the only one whose colors keep their meaning, "12 or more pitches", and so its map changes: no cell reaches 12, and the darkest class is empty.

Neither behavior is wrong, but a reader who compares two maps needs the same color to mean the same thing. The robust fix is to divide by area first: color pitches per square foot, and then threshold breaks mean the same thing at any cell size.

7Context, size and text

A color does not have a fixed appearance. What surrounds it, how big the mark is, and what is written on it all change how it is read.

Simultaneous contrast

The six small squares below have exactly the same fill, #808080. Press the button to remove the gradient.

The square on the dark side looks lighter than the one on the light side. The same thing happens in a heatmap, where a cell's neighbors shift its apparent value, and it is one more reason not to ask readers to read exact values from color: label the cells, or provide a tooltip.

Small marks need stronger colors

The smaller the mark, the harder it is to see its color, and light, low-chroma colors fade first. The same three palettes on bars, dots and thin lines:

Pastel1 works for bars and nearly disappears in dots and lines. Dark2 works everywhere but is heavy on large areas. A good rule: light, soft colors for large areas, darker and more saturated ones for points, lines and text.

Text on colored backgrounds

Labels inside cells need contrast. The WCAG contrast ratio compares the relative luminance of text and background; 4.5 is the minimum for normal text. Below, each step of d3.schemeBlues[9] with dark and white text, and each ratio.

Somewhere in the middle the better ink flips from dark to white, and near the flip neither passes by much. Examples on these pages use a simpler rule, dark text when the background's L* is above about 55–60 and white otherwise, which picks the better ink in nearly every case.

8Color vision deficiency

About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women of Northern European ancestry have a red–green color vision deficiency; rates are somewhat lower elsewhere. In a lecture hall of 100 students, expect several. The main types are named after the cone that is missing or altered:

  • Protanopia and deuteranopia (long- or medium-wavelength cones): reds and greens become hard to tell apart, and red looks dark to protanopes. By far the most common.
  • Tritanopia (short-wavelength cones): blues and greens, and yellows and pinks, merge. Rare.

Most people with these conditions are anomalous trichromats, with a milder version of the same confusions. Simulating the full dichromacy, as the View as menu and the helper cvd(color, kind) do, shows the worst case.

Checking a palette

Simulate each color, then measure how far apart the closest pair is. Here distance is ΔE, the straight-line distance in CIELAB. A ΔE of about 2 is barely noticeable for large patches side by side; for small marks in a chart, Szafir (2018) found that differences need to be several times larger, so treat anything under 10–15 as a warning.

The red-and-green palette collapses for protanopes and deuteranopes, as expected, and so do the first five colors of Category10, whose green, orange and red merge. This site's pitch colors hold up under protanopia but come close to the line under deuteranopia and tritanopia, which is one reason the charts on these pages also separate pitch types by position. Okabe–Ito stays above the line for all three, at the cost of a yellow that is hard to see on white. Every palette has a weakest pair, underlined; do not put those two next to each other. Palette designers automate exactly this test. Note that ΔE in CIELAB is only an approximation of perceived difference, and the 15 used here is a rule of thumb, not a standard.

Replace one color in pitchColor to raise its worst ΔE under deuteranopia without lowering it under normal vision. Is there a trade-off between the two?
One solution

The weakest pair under deuteranopia is the sinker's green and the curveball's pink, ΔE 12.9. Replacing the pink with brown #8c564b raises the worst deuteranopia pair to 18.2 and leaves the worst normal-vision pair unchanged at 37.6. Black does even better on those numbers, 21.4, and a dark violet does worse under normal vision.

The trade-offs do not show up in ΔE. Brown and black are much darker than the other four colors, whose L* runs from 50 to 72, so the curveballs would look heavier and more important than the rest. And a fix for one kind of vision can break another: cyan #17becf also raises deuteranopia to 21.4, but drops tritanopia from 13.8 to 10.6. That is why the solution reports all four. Palettes are designed as a whole, and a single-color fix usually needs a second encoding as well, such as the symbols of example 3.3 of the marks page.

The red–green diverging scale

d3.interpolateRdYlGn is popular because red and green feel like "bad" and "good". It is also the scale that fails for the most readers. Here is the strike-rate map from example 5.1 with RdYlGn and with PuOr, and both simulated for deuteranopia.

Simulated, RdYlGn's two arms turn into similar olive-yellows: the chart no longer says which side of 50% a cell is on. PuOr's purple and orange stay distinct. When a convention demands red and green, make the two arms differ in lightness too, and add a second cue such as labels or symbols.

Simulation is a check, not a guarantee. Beyond picking a safe palette: never make color the only way to read something important. Direct labels, shapes, position, and a table or tooltip with the numbers make a chart readable for everyone, including in grayscale print.

9Fix this chart

This chart shows the share of pitches that batters swung at, by location. It works in the sense that it runs. Find at least six problems with its use of color and scale before you open the solution. Use the View as menu.

List the problems, then fix them in the code above. Consider: the kind of scale, its domain, the legend, the text on the cells, the cells with very few pitches, the strike zone outline, and how the chart looks under each View as option. Add a title that says what a cell's value means.
Show the problems and one possible fix
  1. Rainbow on a quantity. No perceptual order, and interpolateRainbow is cyclical: the lowest and highest rates are both red.
  2. Domain from the data's extent. A rate has natural bounds, 0% and 100%; stretching the colors to the observed range makes small differences look large.
  3. No legend and no title. The reader cannot decode the colors or know what "rate" means.
  4. Black text everywhere. Unreadable on the dark blue and purple cells.
  5. Every cell is as loud as every other. A cell with one pitch shows 0% or 100% in full color, next to cells with thirty pitches.
  6. A red outline on red cells, and red–green confusions in the rainbow for about 1 in 12 men.
  7. Swing rate has no meaningful midpoint, so a diverging scale would be wrong too. A sequential scale fits.

Other reasonable fixes: d3.interpolateViridis instead of Blues, a binned d3.scaleThreshold at 25/50/75%, or a small multiple per pitch type. Check whichever you choose with View as.

Make your own chart from the pitch data that needs color to answer a question neither page asked. Write down, before coding, which job the color does, what kind of scale that implies, and the domain. Check it in grayscale and under deuteranopia before you call it done.
A worked example

Question: where in the zone did batters hit the ball hardest? Color's job: quantify average exit velocity per location cell. Kind of scale: sequential, because exit velocity has no meaningful middle; binned, because baseball already has a meaningful break, 95 mph, which Statcast calls a hard-hit ball. Domain: breaks at 70, 80, 90 and 95 mph, chosen for meaning rather than from the data's extent. Cells with fewer than three batted balls are left out.

Only 20 cells have three or more batted balls, and their averages run from 60 to 96 mph; the one hard-hit cell, in the upper half of the zone toward the catcher's right, rests on 5 balls. Checked with View as: OrRd is a single-hue ramp in lightness, so it survives grayscale and all three simulations, and the printed numbers mean no one has to decode the color at all.
